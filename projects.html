QCIDS - Quantum-Classical Feature Selection for Intrusion Detection code →

Intrusion detectors are usually trained on every feature a flow exporter emits, which is slow and often redundant. I wrote that choice as a QUBO, where each feature is a binary variable and the objective trades relevance against redundancy, then solved the same problem four different ways so the comparison would be fair: QAOA at depths 1, 2 and 3, simulated quantum annealing, and the classical selectors people actually use, from greedy mutual information to JMIM, LASSO and RFE. Every solver hands its subset to the same LightGBM and random forest classifiers, under the same leakage-free cross-validation with SMOTE applied inside folds, so any difference in macro-F1 traces to the features that were picked and nothing else. Three datasets, two of them sharing a NetFlow schema so subsets can transfer between them directly.

Tighten the budget to 15 and greedy selection walks into a trap. It keeps three features that score well overall but carry almost nothing for the Ransomware class, whose F1 falls to 0.48. Searching over combinations instead of ranking one feature at a time avoids this and puts Ransomware back at 0.82 (paired test, p = 0.045). Two things to be clear about: some classical selectors escape the trap too, so this is about combinatorial search rather than anything quantum, and on the other two datasets no solver pulls ahead.

CovNCG - Training-Free Dense Quantum Feature Maps code →

Quantum kernels usually need a circuit someone designed by hand and then tuned, and they fail in a specific way: as the feature map gets deeper the kernel values concentrate, every pair of points looks equally similar, and the classifier learns nothing. CovNCG avoids both problems. Each block packs three features into two qubits using generators that do not commute, which keeps the map expressive without extra depth, and the variance of the resulting kernel has a closed form. That formula is the useful part, because it turns bandwidth selection into something you compute rather than guess, and it tells you in advance where concentration will set in. No training and no architecture search, evaluated with a projected kernel and an SVM on five small medical datasets.

Q-PlasmaNet - Hybrid Classical-Quantum Classifier code →

A frozen CNN does the feature extraction and a small variational quantum circuit of four to eight qubits sits on top as the classifier, with data re-uploading so the same features are fed in at several layers. The question I wanted to answer was narrow and falsifiable: at an equal parameter budget, can a quantum head match a classical one? That framing matters, because most quantum machine learning comparisons put a small quantum model against a large classical one and report the difference as though it came from the quantum part. So the baseline here is an MLP with the same parameter count, alongside full CNNs that are thousands of times larger. Everything runs over five seeds with patient-level splits, and calibration is reported next to accuracy.

PCMMD - Federated Learning and Knowledge Distillation for Plasma Cell Detection code →

Telling plasma cells apart from other cells in bone marrow crops, for multiple myeloma diagnosis. I ran the same task four ways to see what each setting actually costs: ordinary centralized training with five-fold cross-validation, few-shot curves that retrain on 5 to 100 percent of the data to find how little is enough, knowledge distillation from an EfficientNet-B0 teacher into a MobileNetV3 student small enough to deploy at the bedside, and federated training with one client per patient, which is the realistic non-IID case for hospital data. FedAvg and FedProx are compared under both IID and patient-partitioned splits. The evaluation was built first: patient-disjoint folds, validation-only model selection, and image-hash de-duplication so no crop can appear on both sides of a split.

Federated training keeps up with centralized training when clients are balanced (0.990 test accuracy), but falls to 0.883 once the client data gets very uneven. Distillation works well here: the small MobileNetV3 student reaches 0.979 F1 against the teacher's 0.985, where the same student without distillation gets 0.948. Splits are patient-disjoint and duplicate images are removed by hash. One caveat worth stating: the federated part is a training simulation, so it does not provide differential privacy.

Direct-WAM - Self-Supervised Denoising for Two-Photon Calcium Imaging code →

Denoising two-photon calcium recordings, where no clean ground truth exists and every method is self-supervised by necessity. The idea is to look at each objective through the bias it leaves behind rather than through its loss curve. From that you get three results: an error floor each objective cannot beat, a condition that tells you when combining several of them helps instead of averaging away their strengths, and a bound on how much of that a distilled student can inherit. Direct-WAM is the model built to test the theory, a 3D U-Net that takes the noisy volume plus twelve precomputed spatiotemporal autocorrelation channels and predicts the clean volume directly, with no kernel, gate or spike decomposition, distilled from a thirteen-way ensemble teacher. The benchmark had to be rebuilt too, because the baseline everyone cites turns out to be several dB weaker than the same method trained properly.
